
# INTRODUCTION: How to Use This Playbook

*Subtitle: A proven fundraising process, pre-seed to Series A. From first list to final wire.*

## Why This Exists

- There's no shortage of fundraising advice, good and bad. Investors on TikTok telling you how to pitch. Twitter threads from people who've never actually closed a round. Blog posts recycling the same surface-level tips.
- For a lot of founders, all this fragmented knowledge contributes to a core problem: fundraising is a black box.
- This playbook changes that. Ten chapters that walk you through a proven fundraising process, with actionable advice and real know-how. Informed by our own experience raising money from top-tier investors like Y Combinator, Pear Ventures, and Founder Collective. From coaching founders through $80M+ in pre-seed and seed rounds. And from the best industry-regarded resources available.

## The Playing Field

- Fundraising rewards relationships. Founders with deep networks get faster meetings, warmer introductions, and more second chances. That's the reality, and this playbook doesn't pretend otherwise.
- The good news: the game is open to everyone. The starting line is in a different place for every founder, but the finish line is the same. Relationships can be built. Networks can be created from scratch. The founders who raise successfully aren't always the ones who started with the best connections. They're the ones who were the most intentional about building them.
- That's what this playbook teaches you to do. Systematically build the access, the relationships, and the credibility you need to raise on your terms.

## Who This Is For

- The process is by and large stage agnostic, pre-seed to Series A. Where the specifics differ by stage, like traction bars, check sizes, or what investors are evaluating, we call it out directly.

## How This Playbook Works

- Three things to know before you start.
- **Progress is milestone-based.** You advance by hitting real conditions, not calendar dates. No week-by-week schedule.
- **Sequence matters.** The biggest mistake founders make is pitching before they're ready. Resist the urge to skip ahead.
- **Speed and quality together.** Founders who bring rigor to their fundraise close faster and on better terms. Cutting corners in the spirit of moving faster leads to regret.

# CHAPTER 1: The Authentic FOMO Framework

## How a Round Gets Raised

- How a round actually gets raised, from filtered list to final wire, and why running it in the right sequence creates real investor urgency.
- Most fundraises follow a familiar arc. Nine stages from building your target list to final close. Typically, this process takes two to five months when run well at pre-seed or seed.
- Phase tracker: 01 Build filtered list, 02 Line up warm intros, 03 Prep materials, 04 Send intros, 05 First pitches, 06 Follow-up pitches, 07 Diligence, 08 Negotiations, 09 Close
- It starts with sourcing: build a filtered list of investors who fit your stage, sector, and geography. Then comes connector work: line up warm introductions through people those investors actually trust. Commonly, these are founders they have already funded. You prep your materials in parallel: deck, memo, data room.
- Then you activate. Fire the intros, run your first pitches, take second and third meetings with the broader partnership. Then you close it out: diligence, references, financials, product review, negotiations on the term sheet, docs signed, wire hits the account.
- There is a lot of nuance and strategy to consider as you go. The first concept is Authentic FOMO.

## What Is Authentic FOMO?

- A common mistake for first-time fundraisers is taking investor meetings sporadically. A few here, a few there. They end up with a trickle of conversations spread over months, never enough volume in one window to generate real urgency.
- The Authentic FOMO Process solves this. It concentrates a high volume of investor meetings into a short window, so urgency builds naturally. It fits right into the fundraising arc: build your target list, line up warm introductions, activate them, and run your meetings. The hinge is when you activate.
- You don't hit send on your double opt-ins until you've banked enough promised introductions to fill the round. Aim for 80 to 120 promised introductions before anything goes out. Then fire everything at once. That's what packs 30 to 50 meetings into two or three weeks.
- Authentic is the key word. No artificial deadlines. No fake scarcity. Just genuine demand, concentrated into a tight window that any investor can feel and verify.
- Comparison cards:
- Hype FOMO (Manufactured): Artificial deadlines, Fake scarcity, Smoke and mirrors, Falls apart under scrutiny
- Authentic FOMO (Real) ✓: Genuine round traction, Real investor interest, Compressed timelines, Gets stronger under scrutiny

## The Four Goals

- The entire playbook is designed to produce four outcomes. If you execute each chapter properly, these happen naturally.
- Assess investors side by side: When multiple investors are moving at the same time, you can compare them directly and pick the best partners for your cap table.
- More control on round timing: You decide when the round opens and closes. The compressed window means you set the pace, and investors move on your timeline.
- Get ghosted less frequently: When investors know others are actively engaged, they stay responsive. Real activity creates real accountability.
- Every conversation is built on trust from day one: Authentic urgency means you never have to bluff, exaggerate, or grovel. The process speaks for itself.
- Authentic FOMO is concentrated in the middle of the process. It starts when you activate intros simultaneously and peaks during diligence and term sheet negotiations. Everything before that is preparation. The quality of your preparation directly determines the intensity of the FOMO you create.
- The chapters build on each other. Skipping ahead means you won't have the foundation to create real urgency when it matters most.
- Phase tracker: 2-6 Preparation, 7-8 FOMO Builds, 9-10 FOMO Peaks

## Hard Filters

- Every investor on your list goes through two passes.Hard filterscull the list (fail one, they're out).Soft filterstier what's left (T1 gets most of your effort, T2 less, T3 almost none). The hard filters get you from a universe of thousands down to a few hundred. The soft filters get you from a few hundred down to a focused 80 to 120.
- Fail any one of these and the investor comes off the list. No exceptions, no maybes. At first, founders find it difficult to embrace this because they feel investors are a scarce resource. After you filter down, you'll find you have far more perfect-match investors than you thought possible.
- An investor's decision is either a yes or a no. You should only pitch investors who can say yes. Every investor you don't disqualify costs you hours of research, intro work, meeting prep, and follow-up. These hard filters shave weeks off what is an exhausting experience.
- Pass 1: Hard Filters (must-pass or remove)
- 1Thesis Fit20%+ of their deals in your sectorIf they don't already invest in your space, they'd have to learn an entire market to back you. Most won't. Small funds: look for at least 3 deals in your space.
- 2Stage Fit15%+ of their deals at your stageStage-mismatched meetings (a Series A fund taking a pre-seed meeting) happen for the investor's benchmarking. They take the meeting to learn, not to write a check.
- 3Geography5%+ of their deals in your marketMost early-stage VCs prefer to be in their portfolio companies' time zone, on their LPs' regulatory map, and able to meet in person. Cross-continent investing is the exception.
- 4Portfolio ConflictsNo direct competitors in their portfolioAdjacent is fine; direct is not. "Direct" means same customer, same problem, same business model. VCs almost never bet against their existing portfolio. Even if they liked you more, they wouldn't fund you over a company they already own.
- 5Investment Velocity1+ deal in the last 6 monthsFunds that have gone quiet are often out of capital, even if they say otherwise. They'll take meetings to look active and never write a check. No deals in 12+ months is a hard fail.

## Soft Filters

- You've cut your list with hard filters. Now you tier what's left. Soft filters don't eliminate investors. They decide where you spend your effort. Tier 1 gets most of it. Tier 2 gets some. Tier 3 is backstop only.
- What "tiering" actually meansT1: passes everythingEnsure you have a very strong introduction. Schedule these pitches early in the pitch period. Roughly 70% of your prep, outreach, and follow-up effort.T2: fails one soft filterWorth pursuing, but don't sacrifice Tier one time. About 25% of your effort and time. Likely to be follow-on investors, as a lead might convince them to overlook a non-perfect fit.T3: fails two or moreBackstop only. About 5%. Pitch them if a connector volunteers, but never go out of your way.
- $Check Size1 to 2% of total fund size per checkUsually, they state check size publicly, but if they don't, a $50M fund probably writes ~$500K checks. Use as a signal, not a rule. Compelling deals still break patterns.
- VValuation Rangeyellow flagIf their recent deals are priced at 2x your expected valuation, that's a yellow flag. Deprioritize, don't remove.
- MTarget Marketsyellow flagB2B vs B2C, enterprise vs SMB. A portfolio that looks all over the map is a yellow flag. You want investors who have a clear pattern that includes you.
- TTraction Baryellow flagIf comparable deals they backed required 10x your current traction, that's a yellow flag. They may be waiting for more.
- LLead Appetitecritical for lead investorsIf you need a lead (typically rounds of $1M+), target investors who've historically led at least 10% of their deals. 20% would be strong. Below 10%, they're likely a follower. When evaluating your overall list, at least 30% of your Tier 1 should be lead candidates. Angels and family offices are almost always follow-on. Don't put them in Tier 1 lead candidates.
- Example: If your spreadsheet has 180 total investors: 80 Tier 1s (30 of which are leads), 30 Tier 2s, and 70 Tier 3s, your working list is 110. The Tier 3s might be the largest group, but they only get 5% of your effort.

## Why Warm Intros Win

- Turn promised introductions into actual intros using the double opt-in system.

## Booking the Call

- Most of your connectors will be Tier 2 (current portfolio founders). Most of your intros will come from these calls. The goal of every call is one specific outcome: walk away with a commitment to forward your blurb to a specific named partner.
- Before you can run the call, you need to book it. Three principles:
- Booking the call
- Get a warm intro to the founder first.Just like with investors, intros earn you credibility. Cold-emailing portfolio founders works less than half as well as a referral from a mutual connection.
- Send materials before the call.Share your deck or one-pager so they can see the company before you talk. Don't make them learn about you on the call itself.
- Frame the ask softly."I'd love your perspective on our approach" is easier to say yes to than "can you intro me to your investors?"

## Running the Call

- What to avoid on the call
- A promise has a specific definition. Most things connectors say qualify.
- After the call, log immediately, promised or not. Note what they said, which investor they committed to, and the date. If you leave a call unsure whether you have a promise, you don't. Log it ashedged, send one follow-up, and if nothing comes back in a week, move on.
- What "promised" actually means
- ✓Be specific about the investor and partnerName the fund. Name the partner. Specificity is what separates a real ask from a vague one.
- ✓Give a concise, confident picture of the companyYou're arming them with context so the intro email writes itself.
- ✓Make the small ask"Would you be willing to forward a short blurb?" Low friction, easy to say yes to.
- ✓Confirm they know the investor wellA weak intro can hurt more than no intro. Qualify the relationship before you ask.
- ✓Bring them valueA useful connection, a sharp insight, genuine feedback on their product. Reciprocity matters.
- ✕Pitching extensivelyYou're asking, not pitching. Keep the company overview tight so you can focus on the ask.
- ✕Asking for the intro before they agree to the blurbThe blurb forward is the first commitment. Build from there.
- ✕Vague "who do you know?" questionsAlways name the investor first. Open-ended questions get open-ended (useless) answers.
- ✕Accepting paid introsPaid intro brokers carry less credibility. Investors recognize the source. More on this in 3.4.
- ✕Vague enthusiasmnot a promise"I'll see what I can do." Or "Send me your blurb and I'll forward to some investors." No named investor attached means no real commitment.
- ✓Explicit, named commitmentthis is a promiseAgreement to forward your blurb to a specific named partner. Connector understands their role. You've confirmed they know the investor well. Logged in CRM with name, investor, and date.

## Why Practice Is Non-Negotiable

- Practice until your pitch is conversational, your numbers are cold, and your objection handling is instinctive.
- You get one first meeting per investor. Practice frees you to read the room instead of remembering what slide comes next. The goal isn't memorization. It's comfort. VCs invest in humans. Presence and energy can't be replaced by memorization, and they can't be learned in front of a mirror.
- First meetings aretypically 30 minutes: ~5 min intros, optional 5-min quick pitch, ~20 min Q&A. The deck sets the content trajectory. Good founders drive the meeting on their terms.
- How meetings evolve: the first meeting is lighter; subsequent meetings go deeper on specific areas; any time a new person enters the room, re-pitch from scratch.

## How Much Practice You Actually Need

- Your pitch doesn't need to be perfect before you start. It needs to be good enough that you're comfortable. So your energy goes into the conversation, not the content. A few solid sessions with honest, experienced feedback is enough to get there. You will iterate through practice, but the most valuable iteration happens during live investor meetings. This is why you schedule throwaway pitches first. Lower-priority investors early in your window to get your reps in before the meetings that matter.
- Who to practice with (in order):Founders who recently closed > advisors with VC backgrounds > peers (early reps only) > AI (objection prep, not a substitute for human practice).

## Key Terms to Understand Before a Lead Comes In

- This isn't about negotiating tactics yet. That's Chapter 10. This is vocabulary. Know it cold before you need it.
- 1Valuation & DilutionPre-money valuation sets the price; dilution is how much ownership you give up. It compounds across future rounds. Model it out before you agree to anything.
- 2Pro Rata RightsInvestor's right to participate in future rounds to maintain their ownership %. Standard at seed. Understand what you're granting and to whom.
- 3Board CompositionWho gets a board seat. At early stages, fight to maintain founder control. Every seat you give away is leverage you don't get back.
- 4Protective ProvisionsInvestor veto rights on specific decisions: fundraises, acquisitions, major spending. Standard, but worth understanding exactly what you're agreeing to.
- 5Option PoolEquity set aside for future employee grants. Often created at raise time, diluting founders before the investor comes in. Negotiate the size carefully.
- 6Liquidation PreferencePayout order if the company is sold. 1x non-participating is standard and founder-friendly. Anything more aggressive (2x, participating preferred) warrants scrutiny.

# CHAPTER 10: Close the Round

## Lead First, Then Fill

- Secure the lead, fill the rest, negotiate with confidence, and get the wire.
- You cannot fill a round without a lead. The lead sets the terms, leads diligence, and signals credibility to every follow-on investor. Getting to the first yes is the hardest part.
- Once you have a lead, the dynamic of every follow-on conversation changes completely. Your Tier 1 list should have been built around lead investors first. Follow-on investors can be engaged earlier. They often want in before the lead is locked so they're not crowded out later.
- With a signed term sheet, you're managing allocation. Not selling the deal. The dynamics shift entirely.
- Close the Round
- Re-engage Tier 2 and Tier 3 pipeline. Investors who passed or went quiet often reverse on a credible term sheet
- Activate angels and small funds. They move faster and are less sensitive to valuation
- Set a clear allocation framework. Know how much room you have and who gets priority
- Create urgency that's real"We're closing the round in X weeks" is more effective when it's true
- 1Valuation is only one termPro rata rights, board composition, information rights, and protective provisions all matter. A high valuation with bad terms can hurt you more than a lower one with clean terms.
- 2Leverage = competitionThis is why you aimed for multiple term sheets in Chapter 9. Without a competing offer, you're negotiating from zero leverage.
- 3Know your BATNAWhat's your best alternative if this deal falls through? Knowing that clearly makes you a better negotiator. Ambiguity breeds concessions.
- 4A solid deal now beats a perfect deal laterOptimize within realistic bounds. Chasing the ideal that may not materialize costs time and credibility.

- Not every raise closes the way it was planned. The honest version of this chapter includes what to do when the process stalls.
