# Script: The Competition

**Placement:** Chapter 3, Communicate Your Story → The Competition (line ~5978 in fundraising-playbook.html)
**Target length:** ~2:00 to 2:10
**Status:** Finalized. Live on page. YouTube ID: kQ8hLILlJEQ (https://youtu.be/kQ8hLILlJEQ).

---

How a founder talks about the competition shows investors their market awareness, intellectual honesty, and strategic clarity. It's a hard balance: competition is both a threat to your company and validation that the problem and market are real.

Start by mapping the field, including the status quo. The single biggest unforced error is claiming you have no competition. Surface direct competitors, indirect ones, and how the problem gets handled today. For most pre-seed companies, the largest competitor is people doing nothing, a spreadsheet, or an internal tool somebody built five years ago. Naming the status quo often does more work than any logo grid.

From there, lead with what makes you different. Durable differentiators beat features, which are easy to replicate. The real edge sits deeper: technology depth, distribution advantage, positioning, team composition, regulatory access, or a unique insight about the customer.

Tie that differentiator back to your unique insight: the contrarian belief about your customer or market that shaped what you built. The strongest narratives sound like this. "Here's what incumbents are doing. Here's what we believe they got wrong, based on specific customer signals. And that's why we built differently to win this segment first." Competition becomes the proof point for the insight.

Once the insight is set, make the defensibility case explicit. Pick one or two classic mechanisms that genuinely apply: economies of scale, network effects, switching costs. Only claim the defensibility you can prove.

Then anticipate the two standard threats. First: what if a well-funded competitor copies your feature tomorrow? Separate the visible feature from the deeper advantage. Anyone can ship a feature, but the technology depth, distribution, and customer insight beneath it take years to build. Second: what stops a big incumbent from waking up? Name the structural reasons: failed past attempts, channel conflict, misaligned internal incentives.

The single test is simple: can the investor explain, in their own words, how you win?
