A VC fund has a fixed lifespan, a fixed pool of capital, and a fixed math problem: own enough of the winners that those winners return the entire fund several times over.

VCs raise capital from limited partners. Pension funds. Endowments. Family offices. Then they have a fixed window to deploy it. A handful of numbers govern how that happens.

The rule of thumb on check size: most investors write checks of roughly one to two percent of total fund size. A fifty million dollar fund averaging one and a half percent lands around seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars per investment.

The other key numbers.

Fund life: ten years standard, with one to three year extensions common.

Active investment period: three to five years. Most baseline portfolios get built in the first one and a half to three.

Ownership target at seed: funds typically aim to own five to ten percent per company at entry. Concentrated funds aim higher. Large portfolios aim lower.

After the active investment period, capital is reserved for follow-on investments in existing portfolio companies. New investments stop.

So target investors whose current fund was raised in the last three years. Funds beyond that window are usually deploying capital into existing portfolio companies, even if they're still taking meetings.
