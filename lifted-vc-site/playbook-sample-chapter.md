## Sub-chapter 1 — Best Practices

**Crumb:** Chapter 3 · Communicate Your Story

**Intro line:** Investors are evaluating two things at once: how well you communicate your specific startup, and whether you have the communication skills to lead a company. The nine habits below land across both. They apply across everything you write and say, from the first cold email to the last close call.

*[Embedded video: "Best Practices intro" — ~2 min walkthrough]*

### Nine best practices

In the live page, these appear in a horizontal carousel, two habits per slide. Each habit has a number, a name, a description, and a paired weak / strong example.

**01. Love your problem.** Make the investor feel it before they see your solution. Specific people, real stories, quantified pain. The problem is the anchor of the whole story, so spend more time here than feels comfortable.
- *Weak:* "Sales teams need a better way to manage their pipelines."
- *Strong:* "A VP of sales told us last week she has 47 leads in HubSpot, no idea which are warm, and her team is calling them in alphabetical order."

**02. Lead with your strongest assets.** Open with the proof points that win the room fastest: capital committed, a flagship customer, breakout traction, or a team credential. Something like "Closed $650K from two operators in the space." Don't bury the lede.
- *Weak:* "Hi, I'm a founder building a B2B platform. Let me start by introducing the team and the company history."
- *Strong:* "$650K closed from two operators in the space, Reformation as our first customer, $40K MRR in eight weeks. Here's how we got here."

**03. Authentic signal over polish.** Investors read the founder as much as the business. AI-assisted or over-polished writing tends to read as inauthentic. Keep your voice in the collateral, the connector outreach, and the emails. AI is fine for back-office work like list building or CRM logging.
- *Weak:* "We are revolutionizing customer engagement through innovative AI-driven solutions that empower brands to deliver personalized experiences at scale."
- *Strong:* "We help DTC brands write emails that don't sound like every other DTC brand. Increasing email list retention by over 6 months."

**04. Reality supports your narrative.** A real customer in a real moment adds depth and weight. Thread real-world moments through the pitch and the conversation shifts from "is this real" to believable. Paraphrase out loud for natural cadence; quote verbatim in writing where the exact words read as evidence.
- *Weak:* "Our customers love the product and tell us it saves them time and headaches."
- *Strong:* "A finance lead told us last week she used to spend Monday mornings rebuilding this report by hand, and now it lands in her inbox Sunday night and she sleeps better."

**05. Upside framing wins.** Present the round as the opportunity. "$2M backed by these names, doubling MRR every quarter" lands stronger than a defensive pitch. Investors tend to react well when they feel they've discovered something special, so frame it that way.
- *Weak:* "We're raising $2M to extend runway and reach our next set of milestones."
- *Strong:* "$2M backs the team that just signed three Fortune 500 pilots in 90 days, with MRR doubling each quarter."

**06. Use plain English.** Strip marketing speak, AI phrasing, and TED-talk cadence. If a non-industry friend wouldn't follow it, rework it until they would. "Disruptive," "table stakes," "completely transform," "AI-powered, full-stack, next-gen" tend to read as fluff.
- *Weak:* "Our AI-powered platform leverages cutting-edge ML to disrupt B2B procurement workflows."
- *Strong:* "Procurement teams email PDFs back and forth all day. We turn it into a database. Each team gets four hours back per week."

**07. Precise numbers, real metrics.** Be specific. "$2.5M round" reads stronger than "raising $2 to 3M." Choose metrics that matter: paying customers, retention, and unit economics carry more weight than signups, downloads, impressions, or pipeline.
- *Weak:* "Raising $2 to 3M. Strong pipeline momentum, 50K+ users in our funnel, doubling growth quarter over quarter."
- *Strong:* "$2.5M round. Twelve paying customers, $42K MRR, 95% gross retention over the last six months."

**08. Present tense.** "We are doing X." "We work with Y." "We are seeing Z." Future tense ("we will," "we plan to") can signal you're waiting for money to act, and tends to discredit what you've already accomplished.
- *Weak:* "We're going to launch with three pilot partners next quarter, and plan to scale our enterprise motion in 2026."
- *Strong:* "Live with three pilot partners. Two are committed to expand. Three more in late-stage diligence."

**09. Attention to detail.** Typos, mismatched numbers across documents, sloppy formatting, and the AI long dash character. All get noticed.
- *Weak:* "MRR shown as $42K on slide 3 and $48K on slide 8. Email says 'raising $2 to 3M,' deck cover says '$2.5M round.' Three AI long dashes scattered through the executive summary."
- *Strong:* "One number per metric, identical across deck, blurb, one-pager, and email. No long dashes anywhere. Names spelled the same way every time."

## Sub-chapter 2 — The Problem

**Crumb:** Chapter 3 · Communicate Your Story

**Intro line:** The problem is what every pitch is built around. The investor needs to internalize it before the rest of the pitch can land.

*[Embedded video: "The Problem" — ~2 min walkthrough]*

### Four elements every great problem statement shares

Experienced fundraisers spend a disproportionate amount of time working on how they pitch the problem. It leads off in most collateral, and is often the first subject of a meeting. The instinct is to rush to the solution because that's the exciting part, but **it's the problem that makes your solution interesting.** The best problem statements share the same four elements. Build all four into your pitch and the investor sees the problem through your eyes.

A two-column grid of four elements. Each card has a number label, a title, and a description.

**Element 01 — A specific scene.** Anchor in a specific subject. A person, team, department, or company in a clear context. Specific scenes tend to land. Investors connect to something they can picture, then extrapolate to the market on their own. The strongest framings draw a clear line: inside the system something works, outside the system it breaks.

**Element 02 — The customer's POV.** Tell the problem the way the customer lives it. Use their language, their texture, their experience. In writing (deck, one-pager, blurb, data room) quote them verbatim; the exact words read as evidence. Out loud, paraphrase. A recited quote reads as stilted in the room, even when the words are real.

**Element 03 — The cost and scope.** Show both the per-customer cost and how widespread the problem is. Hours burned, dollars lost, deals missed, customers churned. The number of subjects feeling the same pain, and what they collectively spend dealing with it. Establishing the scope here also helps clarify your market size later in the pitch.

**Element 04 — The band-aid solutions.** Walk through what customers do today to manage the problem. Hacky spreadsheets, dedicated headcount, third-party tools that almost work, manual workarounds that break at scale. The silliness of those band-aids does double duty. It proves how badly customers want a fix, and sets up the wedge for your solution.

### Where problem pitches flatten

A warning panel at the bottom of the sub-chapter surfaces the most common failure modes:

- **Stacking multiple problems.** Three problems in the opener usually means you haven't picked the real one. Pick the biggest, lead with it. The others surface naturally inside the solution/features.
- **Naming the problem instead of detailing it.** Specifics make the problem real. "On-call is painful" just names it. "An engineer wakes up at 2am to an alert, opens four dashboards to find the broken service, pages the wrong team, and waits 20 minutes for a human" lets the investor live it.
- **Leading with the diagnosis.** "A categorically broken distribution model" tells the investor your conclusion before they've earned it. Show the moment instead: "I have twelve distributors, each takes 30%, none of them share inventory data, and I find out a week late that I'm out of stock on 30% of my SKUs." Now the investor feels what's broken; the label can come later.
- **Forced personas.** Personas you invent for the slide read as invented. "Meet Jim, who loves movies but hates the video store" lands flat. Use a real customer's words, or skip the persona.
- **Statistics-first opening.** Stats-first reads like a research brief, not a pitch. "The commercial HVAC dispatch market is $11B annually" lands flat. "A rooftop unit at a Walmart fails on a Tuesday afternoon. A dispatcher at a 12-tech shop has 90 minutes to find someone, gets two voicemails, and Walmart calls a competitor instead" lands hard. Open with the scene; the stats earn their slot after.

### Sub-chapter 3 — Why Now

**Intro line:** Why Now tells the investor why this moment matters, and why waiting costs them. The strongest answers are specific, dated, and built on one of three foundations before stacking a second.

**Patterns used:**
- Three-foundation framework grid (Cost dropped / Tech arrived / Buyer changed) with descriptions and quick examples.
- A "Skipped past vs. Lands" callout showing vague language vs. specific language.
- A stacking demo — three nested comparison cards that progress from "One foundation · fragile" → "Two foundations · credible" → "Three foundations · undeniable." Each card shows the same Why Now statement at increasing specificity, plus a fictional investor's reaction.
- A draft-your-Why-Now section with a wizard CTA on the left and a fill-in-the-blank template on the right.
- An "Extra credit" two-up framework grid (Name the graveyard / Pin the window).
- A warning panel listing six common failure modes (every-trend listing, future-tense tailwinds, played-out tailwinds, generic Why Nows, handwaving, urgency-free trends).

## What this sample tells the designer

- **Voice.** Founder-to-founder, specific, plainspoken. Concrete numbers, named scenes, real customer quotes. No corporate jargon, no defensive hedging.
- **Structure.** Each sub-chapter follows a repeatable shape: short intro line, video walkthrough, named framework with named components, supporting prose, worked examples (often expandable), warning panel for common failure modes.
- **Density.** Sub-chapters are substantive but scannable. Bold lines, framework labels, and weak/strong pairs let a reader skim in 60 seconds; the body rewards a closer read.
- **Patterns to design.** Carousels (Best Practices), framework grids (2-up, 3-up, 4-up), expandable example modals, warning panels, stacking demos (Why Now), fill-in-the-blank templates, video walkthroughs, prose with pull quotes. These patterns repeat across the playbook; designing them well here scales the system to all 11 chapters.
